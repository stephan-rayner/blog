## Trading in Trust: How Documentation Protects People, Not Just Projects

Talk outline for Write the Docs Berlin, 2026.

### Opening: The Vacuum

- Hook: documentation isn't a reference artifact, it's trust infrastructure.
- The claim up front: docs don't just inform, they create and preserve the conditions under which people can work together honestly.
- What happens without it: power fills the vacuum, and the loudest voice wins.
- Set up the two stories as opposite ends of the same dynamic, one where the absence of documentation let power win, one where documentation itself was the power.

### Story One: Head of Data at a Toxic, Now Defunct Mobile Games Company

- Set the scene: my role, the team of junior analysts, the stakeholders who wanted data.
- The pattern: stakeholders lean on the newest or most junior person on the team, because they're the easiest to pressure.
- A concrete beat or two: what "misused" looked like in practice (a specific ask, a specific consequence). Pick one memorable incident rather than a general description.
- The absence that mattered: there was no documented standard for what data could be shared, in what form, or why. Every request became a negotiation an analyst had to win alone.
- Land the point: without a written standard to point to, my analysts had no cover. The org chart, not the policy, decided who won.

### Story Two: The Arrogant Senior ML Engineer Who Lost to an Expense Policy

- Tone shift: this one's on me. Set up the seniority and the confidence.
- What I wanted, what the policy said, why I argued anyway.
- The moment the argument actually ended: not because someone outranked me, but because the document held a line I couldn't out-argue.
- Land the point: the policy didn't need a person behind it to win. It won on its own, because it was well-reasoned and written for exactly this moment.

### The Mechanism

- Name it directly: good documentation externalizes authority.
- What that means: it levels a playing field otherwise decided by power, clout, or seniority.
- The payoff: it does this without requiring a difficult conversation every single time. The document has the conversation so the person doesn't have to.
- Bridge: here's how to write documentation that actually does this work, not just documentation that happens to exist.

### The Framework

**Write for the enforcer, not just the reader**
- Why: the reader wants to know what to do. The enforcer has to hold the line when someone pushes back. Those are different jobs and different documents.
- Tie back: my analysts were enforcers with nothing to enforce. The expense policy worked because someone had clearly written it with the pushback in mind.
- How to apply: before publishing, ask "if someone argues with this, what does the person enforcing it say next?" If there's no good answer, the doc isn't done.

**Anchor to mission rather than procedure**
- Why: procedure only covers the cases someone thought of. Mission lets people reason from it when the case isn't covered.
- Tie back: what a data-sharing standard anchored to "why we protect user data" would have given my analysts that a checklist wouldn't.
- How to apply: state the mission near the top, above the steps, not buried in an appendix.

**Document the why alongside the what**
- Why: rules without reasoning invite argument, because an unreasoned rule looks arbitrary, and arbitrary rules feel challengeable.
- Tie back: the expense policy won because it justified itself, not because it was strict.
- How to apply: for every rule, one sentence of reasoning nearby. If you can't write that sentence, question the rule.

**Make your limitations visible**
- Why: a doc that names what it doesn't cover builds more trust than one that oversells, because it's honest about where judgment still has to kick in.
- Tie back: overselling a policy's scope is exactly what leaves an enforcer exposed when a case outside it comes up.
- How to apply: add an explicit "this doesn't cover" or "out of scope" section instead of implying completeness.

### Close

- Return to the vacuum from the opening: documentation is one of the few tools that fills it without a person having to spend their own credibility every time.
- Final line idea: trust isn't something you ask people to extend to you, it's something you can write down and hand them instead.
